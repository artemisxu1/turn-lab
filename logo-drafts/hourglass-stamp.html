The logo — your artwork, white stamp border

Your two PNGs in a pure white perforated border, with the turn animated. Click any of these to invert the hourglass. The spin runs first; the moment it lands, the lower bulb wipes in downward from the neck, so the differentiated cells appear in the order they would have fallen. Nothing cross-fades mid-turn, so there is no ghosting and no double-image — the frame and the upper bulb never move.

The border is a ring now rather than a filled panel: no background, just the perforated edge, the hourglass and the wordmark. Worth knowing what that costs — the hourglass is white line art, so the logo needs a dark surface under it. On a white page it would disappear. If you need one that survives any background, the line art has to gain a dark outline or the panel has to come back.

Two things I changed about the files. I cropped both to their ink: as supplied, the hourglass sat 3.5% left of centre in one canvas and 3.5% right in the other (they are a 180° pair, so that follows), which would have made it slide sideways inside the border as it turned. And your description is exact rather than approximate — spinning before by 180° matches after with a maximum channel difference of 0 across 369,526 inked pixels, which is what lets the transition be genuinely seamless. The cropped copies are images/logo-stamp-before.png and images/logo-stamp-after.png; your originals are untouched.

One caveat I should be straight about: this version is a raster, so it cannot do the jobs a vector mark does. At 32 px the hourglass is about 16 px wide and the cells turn to coloured mush, and it can't be printed in one colour, embossed, or cut as vinyl. For the full-size logo on the site, on slides and in print, the artwork is the right call. For the favicon and the nav mark I'd keep a simplified vector alongside it — Option D below is already built for exactly that, and can be re-cut to match this artwork once you've settled on it. Drawn version — Option C, Engraved

The most literally “old stamp” of the four: printed on warm paper in one ink, the way a real intaglio commemorative was, with a cancellation postmark struck across the corner. The six fates are told apart by shape rather than colour, with a single carmine reserved for the nuclei — which means this version survives a one-colour print, an embossing die, and a laser-cut sticker. The cells lose their colour variation here on purpose: an intaglio press only had the one plate.
